# A place to begin

A useful notebook leaves room for doubt.[^room] I keep mine beside a shallow tray, where a smooth stone stops the pages lifting in a draught. The stone has no special history. Its value is that it stays where I put it.

The first entry describes the shelf itself: pale wood, a dark knot, and a line of dust behind the tray.[^dust] These details are ordinary enough to overlook. Writing them down changes the pace of looking, though it does not make the shelf more important than it was.

I write “perhaps” when an explanation arrives before the evidence.[^perhaps] A curled leaf might have dried in sunlight; it might also have been curled when it came indoors. The sentence can hold both possibilities without choosing between them.

## Three habits

- Name the thing before describing its mood.[^names]
- Separate what happened from what might explain it.[^distance]
- Leave a blank line before revising a first impression.[^blank]

These are habits, not rules for winning an argument. On a hurried morning I may forget all of them and write only: rain, cold handle, sleeve caught on the latch. That small record is still useful.

# Light across the room

By the afternoon, a narrow band of light reaches the tray.[^light] The stone throws a short shadow across the paper. I move neither object, because a moving observer already introduces enough change.

The page is cream rather than white, a difference that is obvious beside the window and almost invisible under the lamp.[^colour] The words remain the same in both places. Their surroundings alter the ease with which I can read them.

A measured interval helps me notice slow changes.[^interval] I look up from the page, count without haste, then look again. This is a way to interrupt distraction, not a substitute for a clock.

> A description can be precise about what it leaves unresolved.[^limits]

That sentence returns me to the first habit of leaving room for doubt.[^room] The return matters: a note is useful only if I can find my way back to the thought that needed it.

## The edge of a shadow

The edge looks sharp until I lean closer. Then I see a soft grey band between the dark centre and the lit paper.[^edge] “Sharp” was not wrong at the original distance, but it was incomplete.

I add the viewing distance to the entry.[^distance] A short description of where I stood can do more work than another adjective. It makes a later observation easier to compare without pretending that the room has stopped changing.

# Keeping a record

I number the leaves of the notebook after writing on them.[^leaves] This avoids treating an empty page as a task that has been left unfinished. A page can wait; the shelf will still be there tomorrow.

When an entry needs correction, I draw a single line through the doubtful phrase.[^correction] I want to read the earlier thought as well as the replacement. The gap between them is part of the record.

An index can be very small:

1. Objects: stone, tray, leaf.
2. Conditions: light, draught, distance.[^index]
3. Questions: what changed, and what did I merely notice later?

I do not copy every entry into the index. Repeated words are useful signposts, but an index that repeats the whole book has lost its purpose.

## When a note is shared

The same explanation may support two different passages.[^shared] I would rather give it one clear home and provide a separate path back to each passage. The reader should not have to remember which page held the original question.

The colour of the paper is one such shared observation.[^colour] Its second appearance does not create a second sheet of paper. It creates another reason to consult the explanation.

# Putting the book away

Before closing the notebook, I read the last entry aloud.[^voice] A missing word is easier to hear when I am no longer supplying it silently. This catches some mistakes, never all of them.

The final line says: “The shelf is still ordinary; my attention is less hurried.”[^ending] I resist adding a lesson. An observation does not need to become advice before it earns its place on the page.

I put the stone back on the tray and leave the notebook open until the ink is dry.[^dry] Then I close it. The next entry will begin with whatever is there, including anything that refuses to fit today's explanation.

There is room for that uncertainty too.[^room]

[^room]: Doubt keeps an observation separate from its possible explanation.

[^dust]: The dust line marks the tray's recent position. It does not establish how long the tray remained there.

[^perhaps]: “Perhaps” records uncertainty. It should not disguise a statement that could readily be checked.

[^names]: A concrete name gives the reader something to locate: a latch, a leaf, or a shallow tray.

[^distance]: Viewing distance affects which details are visible. Recording it helps comparisons made from another position.

[^blank]: A blank line separates the first account from a later response without erasing either one.

[^light]: The band of light is described relative to the tray, not to a measured compass direction.

[^colour]: Cream and white are ordinary colour descriptions here. No colour instrument or calibrated surface was used.

[^interval]: Counting provides a pause of roughly repeatable length. It does not produce a calibrated time measurement.

[^limits]: An unresolved description can still identify the object, the conditions, and the limits of the observer's knowledge.

[^edge]: The soft border of the shadow becomes easier to see at a shorter viewing distance.

[^leaves]: A leaf is a physical sheet. In this notebook, numbering refers to the written side being discussed.

[^correction]: Keeping a correction visible records a change in judgement. It is not a claim that every earlier phrase is reliable.

[^index]: Conditions describe the setting of an observation. Keeping them beside objects makes later comparisons easier.

[^shared]: A shared note needs a distinct return destination for every referring passage; one return path cannot identify several starting points.

[^voice]: Reading aloud changes the way a sentence is reviewed. It complements a visual check of spelling and punctuation.

[^ending]: The last line records the writer's pace of attention, not a measured change in the room.

[^dry]: Closing a wet page can transfer ink to the facing sheet. Waiting protects the record without adding anything to it.
